Mash: what we built

6 October 2026 · 35 slices of work in six days, 30 September to 5 October 2026

What I set out to do

Build my own studio, on my own Mac, that turns my script and voiceover into an animated, roughly ten-minute YouTube video. Its pipeline draws on the many AI video studios and editors I reviewed (see the credits), but it runs on my own keys: Claude, on my subscription, does the thinking; Google's image and video models make the pictures and clips; nothing costs money until I press a clearly priced button; and nothing plays in the video until I've approved it.

A personal note: visual and audio entertainment is changing fast. Once the cost of visual animation comes down, people have incredible imaginations to bring to it. YouTube automation is where this starts for me, not where it finishes.

In short

  • Seven steps, built in six days. Script, storyboard, characters, places, scenes, edit and render: 35 slices between 30 September and 5 October.
  • Claude does the thinking. It writes the storyboard, reviews the pictures and clips, and picks which scenes to animate, from my terminal or from buttons in the app.
  • Every paid call is priced and logged. Pictures and clips come from Google or Fal AI, whichever I choose; each call's cost is recorded, and a retried job never pays twice.
  • The same person and the same room in every picture. Each cast member's approved portrait, and each place's approved picture, goes with every scene they're in; naming someone in an edit brings their portrait.
  • The first real video is under way. All 146 scenes of Surviving a Year After an EMP are drawn; next come Claude's review, the clips, the sound and the render.

Mash was built in slices: each one a small, finished piece, built, tested and approved before the next began. This page shows what the 35 slices added, how the parts fit together, and the week's main problem: making the same person and the same room look the same in every picture.

The seven steps

Every video goes through the same seven steps, each a page in the app, each ending with something to approve.

StepWhat happens
1 · ScriptThe voiceover and its word timings come in, and the narration is cut into scenes of 3 to 6 seconds.
2 · StoryboardClaude writes each scene's picture, camera move, motion and sound, plus the cast and the recurring places. I read, edit and approve it.
3 · CharactersThe look of the video (a drawing style), and one approved portrait per cast member.
4 · PlacesOne approved picture per recurring place, such as the main character's kitchen.
5 · ScenesEvery scene's picture, and the short video clips for scenes that move, made in bulk and approved.
6 · EditA timeline: pictures and clips, music, sound effects and subtitles.
7 · RenderThe finished MP4 and its captions file.

One look: the same person, the same room

On 4 October, while drawing the video Surviving a Year After an EMP, the people and rooms changed from one picture to the next. Image models draw each picture fresh, so anything the instructions don't pin down comes out differently each time. Eight slices over two days gave every person, and every room, a single source to be drawn from.

People: one cast, drawn the same way

S16 hands, feet and hairS16b renameS16c mittens, dressed

Nothing said how hands, feet or hair are drawn, so every portrait invented its own: white mitts, black blobs, skin-coloured hands, stubble, grey wisps. Now the style sets them for everyone, the main character became Jack as the channel describes him, and every description says what's worn below the waist.

Places: one kitchen, every time

S17 places in the storyboardS17b the Places stepS17c scenes use their place

A person had a portrait to copy, but a room had only words, so each scene drew a new kitchen. Now the storyboard names its recurring places (twelve in this video), each gets one approved picture, and every scene set there is drawn with it.

Names: scene 140, “make this Jack”

S18 names in editsS18b one source per thing

A picture can be fixed by circling part of it and saying what to change. But that edit sent only the picture and my words, so the model had never heard of Jack. Naming someone now brings their portrait along, and the style's example pictures stay out of edits, so the people in them can't leak in.

How it all connects

What goes into Mash, the two halves that run it, what it pays for, and what comes out. Everything in the grey band runs on the Mac. The web app is the part you click through; the worker runs the long, often paid, jobs in the background, one at a time.

Worth remembering
  • The word-timings file from mash-voiceover is a contract: if its format changes, Mash's reader has to change with it.
  • Rendering needs the web app running: the renderer fetches the pictures and sound from it.
  • The worker does one job at a time, so a long Claude task holds up paid jobs behind it.
  • A paid job never retries by itself. Retrying it by hand skips what it already made, so nothing is paid for twice.
  • There's no automatic switch to the other provider: a provider that fails, fails, and says why.
  • The tests never pay: they use stand-ins for Google and Fal AI.

One picture's journey

What happens between clicking Draw and an approved scene, and why the pictures come out the way they do.

  1. 1 · I click Drawon the Scenes step, for a range of scenes. The price is on the button, and a paid button never moves under the cursor.
  2. 2 · A job is queuedwith one attempt only: an automatic retry would pay again.
  3. 3 · The worker picks it upone job at a time, then each scene in turn, showing its progress. Cancel stops it between scenes.
  4. 4 · The prompt is builtthe same way for every scene: shot, subject, action, location; “This is Jack's kitchen, drawn the same in every scene set there…”; the people in it; the style.
  5. 5 · The pictures go with itin order, each numbered in the prompt: the style's example pictures, for the look only; the portraits of the people in the scene; the place's picture, last.
  6. 6 · Google or Fal AI draws itwhichever is chosen in Settings. If it fails, say over billing, the job stops and says why.
  7. 7 · Logged and keptits price in the cost log, the picture in the project's folder, a new version waiting for approval, and a key recording what it was drawn from.
  8. 8 · I approve itor change it first, by a sentence or by circling a part. Naming someone (“make this Jack”) brings their portrait along.
Claude's review, optional and freeClaude compares each picture with its storyboard, the portraits and the place's picture, and flags what's off. A flag's note can be used as the edit.
Out of dateIf the storyboard, a portrait or the place's picture changes, the key no longer matches, and the scene is marked out of date, to redraw.

What's next

For this video: approve the 146 pictures (after Claude's review), make the clips, add music and sound effects, and render.

For Mash: next is one command that runs the whole pipeline and asks me at each gate. Later, maybe: gentle 3D movement for still pictures, more subtitle styles, thumbnails and Shorts, and getting the script's visual notes straight into the storyboard, so a future video casts Jack by name from the start.
